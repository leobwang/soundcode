\section{Results}\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Setup}\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Base model.}
Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct \citep{hui2024qwen25coder}, served by
vLLM 0.20.x with bfloat16 weights and a 4096-token context. Greedy
decoding at $T\!=\!0$, $\code{max\_tokens}\!=\!512$ per generate
call (a problem that rolls back restarts generation and may emit
more than 512 tokens in total), with a per-problem
wall-clock budget of $60$\,s. The 60\,s cap is on the entire
problem, including verifier wall time; over-budget problems are
counted as failures.

\paragraph{Benchmarks.}
MultiPL-E HumanEval-rs and HumanEval-cpp
\citep{cassano2022multipl,chen2021humaneval}. The source benchmark
has 164 problems; MultiPL-E ships $156$ Rust and $161$ C++
translations (problems that do not translate cleanly are dropped by
the benchmark), and we use every shipped problem.
For each problem, the prompt is the function signature plus a
docstring describing the task; the completion is the function body.
We evaluate pass@1 by running the unit tests packaged with each
problem against the generated body.

\paragraph{Hardware.}
A single workstation with one NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell
(96\,GB VRAM) for the LM and a 16-core CPU for the verifier.
\code{cargo check} runs on a single core in incremental mode with
the workspace warm. We pre-warm cargo on each language so the first
verifier call of a session is not slower than the steady state.

\paragraph{Arms.} The full design space factors into nine arms
across the scheduling choices, the rollback policies, and the
verifier: \textbf{Vanilla} (no verifier), \textbf{SyncCargo}
(synchronous, ROCODE-style) and \textbf{AsyncCargo} (SoundCode)
each crossed with the \textsc{NaiveLast} / \textsc{RecBackoff} /
\textsc{NaiveLast+Penalty} rollback policies (all with
\code{cargo check} / $g{+}{+}\;\code{-fsyntax-only}$ as verifier),
plus \textbf{AsyncLSP} (rust-analyzer via \code{multilspy} as
verifier, \textsc{NaiveLast} policy) and \textbf{ROCODE-upstream}
(the published ROCODE implementation).

Of these nine, the Phase-1 grid reports \textbf{four}, each run on
both languages: Vanilla, SyncCargo\,+\,\textsc{NaiveLast},
AsyncCargo\,+\,\textsc{NaiveLast}, and
AsyncCargo\,+\,\textsc{RecBackoff}. Every Phase-1 measurement
(Table~\ref{tab:main} and \S\S\ref{sec:main-results}--\ref{sec:results-ablation-policy})
is therefore \emph{compiler}-supervised, and the Phase-1
rollback-policy ablation compares \textsc{NaiveLast} against
\textsc{RecBackoff} only. A post-deadline follow-up
(\S\ref{sec:extended}) adds the arms the original scoping cut:
the fully-wired \textsc{ROCODEEntropy} policy (async, both
languages), AsyncLSP plus a SyncLSP companion (rust-analyzer as
verifier), all four \textsc{NaiveLast+Penalty} arms, a Java third
language, and a matched-budget resampling baseline outside the
original nine. Still unevaluated at this revision:
SyncCargo\,+\,\textsc{RecBackoff} and ROCODE-upstream (skipped for
the separate reason documented in \S\ref{sec:results-rocode}).

\paragraph{Metrics.}
pass@1, mean wall-clock time per problem (seconds), mean rollback
count per problem, and compile rate (whether the final buffer
\code{cargo check}-passes / $g{+}{+}\;\code{-fsyntax-only}$-passes).
We additionally report \emph{verifier idle time} (seconds spent
awaiting a verdict that already blocks decoding) for the sync arms
and the corresponding \emph{producer wait fraction} for the async
arms, to make the saved wall-time directly attributable to async
overlap.

\paragraph{Runner revision (v2).} During the follow-up we found a
bug in the boundary detector's firing logic: because trailing
whitespace hides the statement terminator, a purely-whitespace
token chunk re-fired the boundary that had already been checked,
re-invoking the verifier (and, in sync mode, aborting the stream)
on the \emph{same} boundary. The tax fell asymmetrically on the
sync arms --- async's single-in-flight-check rule absorbs
re-fires --- inflating sync wall-times by roughly $24\%$ on Rust
and $46\%$ on C++
and overstating the async advantage. All numbers in this revision
come from the corrected runner (v2). Removing the spurious aborts
also shifts a modest number of per-problem outcomes through
altered rollback interactions (most visibly sync-C++, where 14
test outcomes change and pass@1 rises from $71.4\%$ to $75.2\%$).
For the record, the
uncorrected runner measured $1.59\times$ (Rust) and $2.95\times$
(C++); the corrected headline below is $1.47\times$ and
$2.16\times$. Both raw datasets are committed.

\paragraph{Statistical note.} All results are from a single greedy
generation per problem per arm ($T\!=\!0$, one seed), so we report
paired-difference significance rather than across-run variance.
By McNemar's exact test on paired problems, the headline
sync-vs-async comparison at matched \textsc{NaiveLast} policy shows
no significant pass@1 difference on either language (Rust:
$3$ vs $10$ discordant, $p\!=\!0.092$, direction favoring sync;
C++: $8$ vs $5$, $p\!=\!0.58$), and no verified arm differs
significantly from \textsf{Vanilla} on pass@1 (closest:
\textsf{SyncCargo}-Rust at $+6$ problems, $p\!=\!0.15$, and
\textsc{RecBackoff}-Rust at $-6$, $p\!=\!0.070$). The only
significant pairwise effects in the grid disfavor
\textsc{RecBackoff} on Rust compile rate (vs \textsf{Vanilla}
$1$~vs~$10$, $p\!=\!0.012$; vs naive-async $1$~vs~$9$,
$p\!=\!0.022$). None
of these effects favors verification over plain decoding.
Accordingly, this paper claims a \emph{latency} improvement only:
the wall-time gaps in Table~\ref{tab:main} are large, consistent
across mean, median, and p90 (p90: $1.97$\,s sync vs $1.14$\,s
async on Rust; $3.72$\,s vs $1.72$\,s on C++), and hold on
$78$--$99\%$ of individual paired problems; the pipeline's
run-to-run stability is characterized in \S\ref{sec:repro}.

\subsection{Headline finding}\label{sec:headline}

\paragraph{C++.} Switching the same rollback algorithm from sync to
async cuts mean per-problem wall-time from $2.50$\,s to $1.16$\,s
($2.16\times$ speedup at the means; $2.20\times$ at the per-problem
median). On $98.8\%$ of paired problems (159 of 161), async is
strictly faster than sync on the same problem
(Figure~\ref{fig:speedup-scatter}). pass@1 is $77.0\%$ async vs
$75.2\%$ sync, a difference of a handful of problems that is not
statistically significant (McNemar exact test on the paired
problems, $8$ vs $5$ discordant, $p\!=\!0.58$), and we do not claim
it as an accuracy effect.

\paragraph{Rust.} The wall-time win on Rust is $1.47\times$
($1.05$\,s sync to $0.71$\,s async; $1.31\times$ at the per-problem
median), and the async arm's overhead over vanilla decoding is just
$1.4\%$ ($0.714$\,s vs $0.704$\,s) --- on Rust, verification is
essentially free under async scheduling. Async beats sync on
$78.2\%$ of paired problems ($122/156$). pass@1 is
$73.7\%$ sync vs $69.2\%$ async; as on C++, the difference is not
statistically significant ($3$ vs $10$ discordant, McNemar exact
$p\!=\!0.092$). The smaller wall-time gain is exactly the
verifier-cost-spectrum prediction of \S\ref{sec:when-async}:
incremental \code{cargo check} on the warm workspace amortizes to
$\sim$\,$0.20$\,s per call in our setup
(\S\ref{sec:headline-contrast}) --- roughly a third of C++'s
per-check cost --- so the sync arm's per-boundary stall is shorter,
leaving less wall-time for the async overlap to recover.

\subsection{Rust vs.\ C++: the verifier-cost spectrum}\label{sec:headline-contrast}

The asymmetry $2.16\times$ (C++) vs $1.47\times$ (Rust) is
predicted by the cost-spectrum analysis of
\S\ref{sec:when-async}: the relative async win scales with the ratio
$c_v / c_d$ of verifier cost $c_v$ to per-token decode cost $c_d$.

Concretely, the sync arm's wall-time decomposes as
$\overline{t}_{\text{sync}} \approx
\overline{t}_{\text{plain}} + c_v \cdot K$, where $K$ is the number
of boundary checks per problem. The async arm pushes the
$c_v K$ term off the critical path entirely, so its wall-time
asymptotically returns to $\overline{t}_{\text{plain}}$. Empirically,
this matches the data on C++: plain C++ is $0.87$\,s and async-C++
is $1.16$\,s (a 34\% overhead from rollback + the end-of-stream
drain), while
sync-C++ is $2.50$\,s, dominated by $K \approx 3.0$ \code{g++}
syntax-only invocations at $\sim$\,$0.55$\,s amortized cost each (the
cold-start time of the C++ compiler is large relative to anything
\code{cargo check} pays). On Rust, plain is $0.70$\,s and async-Rust
is $0.71$\,s (a 1.4\% overhead); the sync overhead is
$\sim$\,$0.35$\,s ($K \approx 1.8$ checks at $\sim$\,$0.20$\,s
amortized incremental \code{cargo check}). Figure~\ref{fig:overhead}
visualizes this decomposition.

\subsection{Ablation: rollback policy}\label{sec:results-ablation-policy}

Within the AsyncCargo arms, the recurrence-gated back-off
(\textsc{RecBackoff}, \S\ref{sec:where-rollback}) does
\emph{not} improve over the simpler \textsc{NaiveLast} policy on
this benchmark: on Rust, \textsc{RecBackoff} is $66.0\%$ pass@1 vs
naive
$69.2\%$ (and compiles significantly less often: $126$ vs $134$,
$p\!=\!0.022$); on
C++, it is $76.4\%$ vs naive's $77.0\%$. Rollback is rare overall
--- $132$ of $156$ Rust problems and $155$ of $161$ C++ problems
never roll back under AsyncCargo-Naive ($0.17$ and $0.06$ mean
rollbacks/problem; Figure~\ref{fig:rollback-dist}) --- and the two
policies diverge sharply on the firing minority: under
\textsc{NaiveLast} a firing problem rolls back once or twice
(median $1$, max $2$) and settles, while under \textsc{RecBackoff}
the extra back-off re-encounters errors and typically thrashes to
the per-problem rollback budget (median $7$ among firing problems,
the budget maximum). The clean read is that the simpler policy
suffices on this benchmark: most problems never exercise the
policy at all, and the more aggressive target actively harms the
minority that do. The same conclusion holds for ROCODE's full
entropy-based target selection, evaluated in
\S\ref{sec:extended}.

\subsection{Extended arms (post-deadline follow-up)}\label{sec:extended}

The arms below were run after the original submission, on the same
model, hardware, benchmark revision, and decoding configuration as
Phase~1; raw records are committed alongside the Phase-1 data.

\paragraph{ROCODEEntropy, fully wired.} We wired ROCODE's complete
target-selection rule into the sampler --- per-token Shannon
entropies from the top-20 logprobs, error-line offset $r_e$ on the
first rollback for an error, max-entropy line $r_h$ on recurrence
--- and re-ran the async arms. The verdict from the
\textsc{RecBackoff} proxy stands. On C++, the wired rule ties
\textsc{RecBackoff} ($123/161$ pass@1) one problem below
\textsc{NaiveLast} ($124$). On Rust it scores
$66.0\%$ ($103/156$) --- identical to \textsc{RecBackoff},
non-significantly below
\textsc{NaiveLast} on pass@1 ($1$~vs~$6$, $p\!=\!0.13$),
significantly below
it on compile rate ($1$~vs~$9$, $p\!=\!0.022$), and significantly
below \textsf{Vanilla} on pass@1 ($0$~vs~$6$, $p\!=\!0.031$).
Entropy-targeted rollback does not rescue the recurrence-heavy
minority on this benchmark; it only relocates the rollback target
inside problems that fail under every policy. (The wired arm's
wall-times, $1.23$\,s Rust / $1.39$\,s C++, are not comparable to
Table~\ref{tab:main}: computing top-20 logprobs slows decoding for
every token.)

\paragraph{LSP verification.} With the repaired rust-analyzer
driver (below), we ran the AsyncLSP arm --- and a SyncLSP
companion --- on Rust. Three results. \emph{First, the LSP tier is
not vestigial once the driver is correct}: blocking diagnostics
fire and roll back ($6$ rollbacks across $6$ problems async;
$15$ across $13$ sync), in contrast to earlier project experiments
in which the LSP tier never blocked. Much of that earlier null
result traces to a driver bug rather than to rust-analyzer itself:
the shipped multilspy-based checker never delivered content
updates the server acted on (rust-analyzer computes rustc-class
diagnostics via its embedded \code{cargo check} only on
\code{didSave}, which the driver never sent), so every check after
the first returned the first check's diagnostics. \emph{Second},
accuracy tracks the compiler arms exactly: async-LSP passes
$108/156$ (async-cargo's count; vs
\textsf{Vanilla} $p\!=\!1.0$),
sync-LSP $115/156$ (sync-cargo's count; vs \textsf{Vanilla}
$p\!=\!0.15$).
\emph{Third}, the sync-vs-async contrast reproduces at LSP-grade
verifier cost: $3.52$\,s sync vs $2.46$\,s async at the means
($1.43\times$; async faster on $128/156$ paired problems, median
per-problem ratio $1.33\times$). Two honest caveats. LSP
verification is strictly dominated by \code{cargo check} here ---
same pass@1 at $3.4\times$ async-cargo's wall-time
($2.46$\,s vs $0.71$\,s) --- and the $1.43\times$ ratio sits
\emph{below} even Rust-cargo's $1.47\times$ despite a per-check
cost roughly ten times higher, which refines the cost-spectrum
model of
\S\ref{sec:when-async}: once a single check ($\sim$$1$--$2$\,s)
approaches the total decode time, the final end-of-stream verdict
--- which async must await for correctness --- cannot be hidden,
and the async win saturates. The prediction ``async pays when
per-check cost exceeds per-token cost'' holds; the win is largest
when per-check cost is also \emph{small relative to total decode
time}.

\paragraph{Model-size scaling.} The cost-spectrum framing
(\S\ref{sec:when-async}) makes a falsifiable prediction: the async
win grows with the ratio of per-check verifier cost to decode
cost, so a smaller, faster-decoding model should show a
\emph{larger} win at the same verifier. We re-ran the four core
arms with Qwen2.5-Coder-1.5B-Instruct (vanilla decode:
$0.20$\,s/problem Rust, $0.23$\,s C++ --- $3.6$--$3.8\times$
faster than
the 7B). The prediction holds on both languages: the sync/async
ratio rises from $1.47\times$ to $1.69\times$ on Rust (async
faster on $126/156$ paired problems) and from $2.16\times$ to
$3.17\times$ on C++ ($159/161$), with pass@1 again statistically
indistinguishable between schedulings (McNemar $p\!=\!0.27$ Rust,
$p\!=\!1.0$ C++). (The 1.5B grid ran on the corrected runner; its
ratios are directly comparable to the v2 headline.) The spectrum's
measured points are ordered exactly as per-check-cost relative
to decode time predicts.

\paragraph{A second benchmark: MBPP.} HumanEval's $n$ leaves
$\pm$$7$\,pp confidence intervals on pass@1; MultiPL-E's MBPP
translations give $2.3\times$ the sample ($n\!=\!354$ Rust,
$n\!=\!397$ C++). Both headline findings survive the added power.
The latency result: sync/async ratio-of-means $1.54\times$ on
Rust (async faster on $273/354$ paired problems) and $2.18\times$
on C++ ($394/397$), at means $0.91$\,s\,$\to$\,$0.59$\,s and
$2.25$\,s\,$\to$\,$1.03$\,s. The accuracy null sharpens rather
than weakens: sync-vs-async pass@1 is non-significant on both
languages ($15$~vs~$10$ discordant, $p\!=\!0.42$ Rust; $11$~vs~$5$,
$p\!=\!0.21$ C++), and async-vs-\textsf{Vanilla} on C++ has
\emph{zero} discordant problems out of $397$ --- the verified arm
passes exactly the problems vanilla decoding passes. The bar any
in-loop verification system must clear
\citep{olausson2024selfrepair} is post-hoc filtering under the
same budget. Our baseline samples complete candidates at
$T\!=\!0.8$ (seeded per sample) and submits the first that
\emph{compiles} --- the compiler as a post-hoc filter rather than
an in-loop supervisor; unit tests are never consulted during
selection. On Rust it is the strongest arm in this paper:
$120/156$ pass@1 ($76.9\%$) at $1.12$\,s mean wall and $1.39$
samples per problem --- significantly above async in-loop
verification on both pass@1 ($20$~vs~$8$ discordant,
$p\!=\!0.036$) and compile rate ($155/156$; $21$~vs~$0$,
$p\!<\!0.001$), and above \textsf{Vanilla} on pass@1 at the edge
of significance ($p\!=\!0.052$). On C++ it trails on pass@1
within noise ($120/161$ vs async's $124$, $p\!=\!0.52$; vs
\textsf{Vanilla}
$p\!=\!0.54$) with a compile rate of $160/161$ (vs async
$4$~vs~$0$ discordant, $p\!=\!0.13$) at higher wall
($1.87$\,s vs $1.16$\,s). Two
qualifications: the baseline decodes at $T\!=\!0.8$ while the
verified arms are greedy, so this is a system-level comparison,
not a one-variable ablation; and it emits $1.4$--$1.5\times$ the
tokens (whole candidates are discarded, not suffixes). The honest
synthesis: \emph{for batch pass@1 on this benchmark,
resample-and-filter is the stronger default on Rust and
competitive on C++}, and the case for
in-loop verification rests on what resampling cannot provide ---
a monotonically growing verified prefix (streaming/interactive
use), bounded per-problem tail latency, and the larger salvage
value of long generations, none of which this benchmark measures.

\paragraph{NaiveLast+Penalty.} We completed the last policy in the
design space by implementing ROCODE's $\lambda^{t-r}$ token
penalty as an engine-registered per-request logits processor
(vLLM V1's subprocess architecture rules out the shared-state
processor the original implementation attempted; the penalty trie
is serialized into each retry request instead). The result is a
null: against the corrected \textsc{NaiveLast} baselines, the
penalty changes pass@1 by at most one problem in any arm (all
McNemar $p\!=\!1.0$; e.g.\ async-Rust $109$ vs $108$, async-C++
$124$ vs $124$) at indistinguishable wall-times. With rollback
firing on ${\sim}4$--$15\%$ of problems and settling within one or
two retries, the mask simply has almost nothing to steer. We note
this expressly because an earlier uncontrolled comparison
(penalty on the corrected runner vs naive on the uncorrected one)
made the penalty look transformative; against the matched baseline
the effect disappears.

\paragraph{Java.} A third language probes the
$\code{javac}$-grade region of the cost spectrum
(MultiPL-E humaneval-java, $n\!=\!158$; \code{javac} in-loop
amortizing to ${\sim}0.2$\,s per invocation). Async verification behaves as
on Rust and C++: $107/158$ pass vs vanilla's $108$, at $0.83$\,s
vs $0.67$\,s mean wall ($+24\%$). The sync arm, however, degrades
outright ($86/158$ pass, $107/158$ compile): its per-boundary
abort-and-restart cycle drifts the model's indentation, nested
closing braces land at the four-space indent level, and
MultiPL-E's indentation-sensitive stop token (\code{\textbackslash
n\ \ \ \ \}}) then truncates the function mid-body --- an artifact
of restart-based scheduling interacting with stop-token
conventions, not of verification. We therefore do not quote a
sync/async ratio for Java; the honest Java result is that
\emph{async verification is accuracy-neutral at modest overhead
where sync scheduling is not even well-defined under the
benchmark's stop-token convention}.

\subsection{Ablation: diagnostic classifier}\label{sec:results-ablation-classifier}

The diagnostic-classifier hyperparameter (\S\ref{sec:when-rollback})
was originally going to be swept against a learned-classifier
baseline (SemGuard \citep{wang2025semguard}); however, a faithful
SemGuard comparison requires retraining the 1.3B classifier on a
curated Rust diff corpus (the released SemGuard model is
Python/Java only), which is out of scope for this paper. We
therefore use a fixed compiler-derived classifier (\textsc{Blocking}
$=$ any \code{error[E\dots]} diagnostic) across all sync and async
arms, and defer the classifier-threshold sweep to the
SemGuard-comparison follow-up described in
\S\ref{sec:conclusion}.

\subsection{ROCODE-upstream baseline}\label{sec:results-rocode}

The ROCODE-upstream arm, a head-to-head against the published
ROCODE \citep{jiang2024rocode} implementation on HumanEval-cpp,
was not runnable in time. The released ROCODE codebase ships a
Python-only program analyzer (\code{PA\_tools.py}, which calls
Python's \code{exec()} and catches \code{SyntaxError} /
\code{AssertionError}); it has no C++ analyzer and no hook to
substitute one. Porting ROCODE's PA to C++ (a g++ syntax-error
parser plus a dynamic test driver) is a multi-day engineering
effort that we judged not worth its share of the wall-clock budget,
since the algorithmic comparison we care about is already isolated
by the \textsf{SyncCargo~Naive} arms. Those arms run ROCODE's
algorithm \emph{exactly}~--- block at every depth-zero structural
boundary, run the program analyzer to completion, classify the
verdict, roll back to the most recent boundary checkpoint on a
blocking error~--- on the same Qwen2.5-Coder-7B model and the same
\code{g++ -fsyntax-only}/\code{cargo check} verifier the async arms
use. The only differences from upstream ROCODE are the language
adapter (C++/Rust rather than Python) and the inference engine
(vLLM rather than HuggingFace \code{transformers}). We document
this scoping decision in
\code{results/paper\_phase1/known\_issues.md}.

\subsection{Producer-wait fraction}\label{sec:results-wait}

Figure~\ref{fig:overhead} decomposes each arm's mean wall-time into
a \emph{decode} term (estimated as the corresponding vanilla-arm
mean) and a \emph{verifier-overhead} term (the residual, including
all serialized verifier wait and rollback wall-time). On C++, the
verifier-overhead share is $65\%$ of sync wall-time and drops to
$25\%$ for async; on Rust, $33\%$ shrinks to $1.4\%$. The async
verifier-overhead is non-zero because (i) the producer must still
await the final verifier verdict before emitting the EOS token (the
correctness invariant of \S\ref{sec:arch}), and (ii) any actual
blocking rollback incurs the discarded suffix's decode time.

\subsection{Reproducibility}\label{sec:repro}

All artifacts needed to regenerate this paper's numbers are in the
repository: the experiment runner
(\code{scripts/paper\_phase1.py}), the analysis script
(\code{scripts/paper\_phase2.py}), the raw per-problem JSONL
records and run log, and a locked Python environment
(\code{uv.lock}: vLLM 0.20.2, torch 2.11.0, datasets 4.8.5). The
model is the stock HuggingFace snapshot of
Qwen2.5-Coder-7B-Instruct; the benchmark revision of
\code{nuprl/MultiPL-E} is pinned in the runner. The full grid runs
in ${\sim}40$ minutes on the hardware of \S\ref{sec:setup}
(\code{uv run python scripts/paper\_phase1.py}, then
\code{scripts/paper\_phase2.py} for aggregates and figures).

\paragraph{Verification and replication.} The pipeline's
repeatability was established on the original (uncorrected) runner
before the v2 revision: a 20-problem-per-language re-execution of
all eight arms matched the stored records exactly on $160/160$
per-problem compile/test/token outcomes with per-arm mean
wall-times within $3\%$, and five independent full-grid executions
agreed to $\pm 0.03\%$ (Rust) and $\pm 0.2\%$ (C++) on the
headline ratio with per-problem outcomes identical across all
five runs --- including the async arms', which are race-dependent
in principle. Raw records of every run, v1 and v2, are committed.
The analysis pipeline is deterministic: re-running it regenerates
the aggregate CSV and report byte-identically and all figures
content-identically.

\paragraph{Determinism caveats.} Plain and sync arms are
token-level deterministic up to vLLM prefix-caching numerics
(greedy $T\!=\!0$, problems served sequentially). Async-arm
outcomes depend in principle on verifier-vs-decoder wall-time
races (whether a verdict lands before token $k$ or $k{+}1$), so
their rollback offsets and generated code reproduce approximately
rather than by construction --- though they reproduced exactly in
the verification above. All wall-time magnitudes are specific to
the hardware and toolchain; the runner logs \code{rustc},
\code{cargo}, and \code{g{+}{+}} versions with each run
(verification used rustc 1.92.0 and g{+}{+} 13.3.0).
